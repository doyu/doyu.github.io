Anora (2024): The Fight No One Records

Sean Baker’s Palme d’Or winner is not about poverty. It is about fighting fair with everything you have, in a world that never writes it down.

Anora (2024) is written, directed, and edited by Sean Baker, starring Mikey Madison. It won the Palme d’Or at Cannes and five Academy Awards, including Best Picture.

Warning: spoilers ahead, including the ending.

My first impression, honestly, was: nice film, good atmosphere — but this swept the awards season? It took me a while to figure out what I had actually watched. This is a record of that figuring-out, because my reading of the last scene changed three times, and the final version changed what I think the whole film is about.

What gets praised

The usual points are real. The film jumps genres without breaking: a Cinderella story in the first act, a screwball manhunt comedy in the middle, a quiet study of loss at the end. It sets up the Pretty Woman fantasy only to refuse it, scene after scene, until the credits. And it treats a sex worker neither as someone to be rescued nor as a tragic heroine, but as a person doing a job and living a life. Mikey Madison carries the entire register — comic velocity to final collapse — by herself.

All true. But none of it explained why the film stayed with me.

The last scene, three times

The ending: in Igor’s car, Ani initiates sex — her way, on her terms. Igor responds by simply being gentle with her, and tries to kiss her. She breaks down crying.

First reading. A happy ending with Igor is too cheap for this film, so it can’t be that. The sex is plain gratitude toward the one man who treated her decently — he protected her, he brought back her ring. The tears are the end of the fight: everything she fought for is gone, and Igor merely provided a place where she could finally cry.

Second reading. Ani’s toughness is armor — a survival strategy, not a personality. She has lived in a world where intimacy is a transaction, and gratitude is a debt to be repaid. The repayment fails in the middle because Igor doesn’t want a transaction. A kiss is mutual; it can’t be performed or controlled. Her control breaks, and the armor comes off for the first time in the film.

Third reading — the one I’ve landed on. Igor is not a savior and not a love interest. But he is a witness. Returning the ring is a small act of acknowledgment: the marriage happened, the humiliation happened, your fight happened. Everyone else in the film — the family, the fixers, the husband — worked to erase what happened to her. Igor is the only one who didn’t. So the tears are not pure despair. They are also this: for one moment somebody saw the fight, and that made it possible to finally stop fighting.

The third reading contains the first two; it doesn’t cancel them. Sean Baker has described the sex differently — as Ani reclaiming power rather than giving thanks — and Madison has called the ending a Rorschach test, declining to fix an interpretation. The openness is the design.

What the film is actually about

Here is the part that took me longest. Ani’s rootlessness is not poverty. The film is careful about this, and the screenplay is explicit: her home is written as ANI’S APARTMENT — she shares it with her sister and pays her own rent. She has an address, an income, competence, and considerable nerve. She is not a person who has nothing.

What she doesn’t have are two other things.

Invisibility. Ani fights fair, with everything she has, and the world does not record it. Vanya’s family never once perceives her as a person; her fight is legitimate, but it was never entered in their ledger to begin with. That is the exact cruelty of the film’s final stretch: not that she loses, but that from the world’s point of view nothing happened at all.

No point of return. She has a place to sleep, but not a place that can receive who she has become. The ending’s quiet horror is that after everything, she goes back to exactly where she started, and the world shows no trace of any of it.

The two are one structure, really: a person who is not recognized cannot have a place whose whole premise is being recognized. That is what this film is about, and it is why the mansion scenes and the money never landed for me while the two things above cut deep.

Where it caught me

I have lived in Finland for more than twenty years as an immigrant. I have a home here and a life here, and still the country itself has never quite become a place of return; meanwhile the country I left has, over twenty years of absence, slowly stopped being one too. Not homelessness — I want to be precise the way the film is precise. A place to return exists; it is just not a place where the whole of you can be found. Fighting fair, being competent, and still not being written into anyone’s ledger — that is the part of Ani I recognized, and it has nothing to do with her bank account.

Takeaway

If the third reading is right, the film carries a usable implication. What Ani lacked was never a place or money. It was a witness. The antidote to invisibility is not blending in — twenty years of trying tells me that has a ceiling — but the presence of someone who sees the fight and does not erase it. The film ends before telling us whether one moment of being witnessed can change anything. That question is left to us, which I suspect is exactly what Baker intended.
